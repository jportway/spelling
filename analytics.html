How to read this

Only first attempts count. When a letter goes in the wrong hole the tile falls back to the pile and she tries again — but now she knows one letter it is not. Counting that second guess alongside the first would quietly flatter her, so every accuracy figure here is built from her first attempt at each hole.

Every rate carries its count. A percentage from eleven attempts is barely information. The ranges shown are 95% intervals: the range the true figure plausibly sits in given how few attempts there have been. Two letters whose ranges overlap have not been shown to differ, however far apart their percentages look.

The game is part of the measurement. She can only put a “d” where a “b” belongs if a “d” was on the table. The puzzle generator offers the confusable partner about nine times in ten when the missing letter is tricky, so raw swap counts are conditional on an offer that is not always made. Where the pool was recorded, the rates are worked out over the times the wrong letter was actually there to pick.

Before and after splits on attempts, not dates. Half her practice on each side, whenever it happened. Splitting on the calendar would put a fortnight's holiday in one bucket and three rounds in the other, then report the difference as progress.

“No detectable change” is not “no change”. It means the data cannot tell the difference yet. With a few rounds a week that is usually the honest answer for months, which is why the summary says how many more attempts a given question would need.

Times include the word being read out. Most words have one hole and she gets most of them first go, so nearly every timing is the opening move — which waits on the speech. That puts a floor of a couple of seconds under all of it. The trend still means something, because the speech costs the same every round.
